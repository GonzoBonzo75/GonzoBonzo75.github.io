Migrant Mother

17 November 2026

On Tuesdays, one photograph. This time I can show it to you.

March 1936, California. Dorothea Lange is driving home after a month working for a US government agency that documents rural poverty. She passes a sign pointing to a camp of pea pickers and keeps going, for some twenty miles. Then she turns back.

The frost has destroyed the crop, and in the camp near Nipomo there are people with no work and no money. Lange stays about ten minutes. She takes a few photographs, moving a little closer each time.

The woman is thirty-two, with three children pressed against her. Lange does not ask her name.

The prints reach the San Francisco News, which publishes them within days. Twenty thousand pounds of food leave Washington for the camp. By the time it arrives, the family is gone.

The photograph becomes the face of the Great Depression. But the woman is Florence Owens Thompson, born in 1903 in Oklahoma into a Cherokee family, living in California since 1926: she was not the refugee everyone saw.

In 1978 a reporter from the Modesto Bee identifies her. Florence says she never earned a cent from that photograph and never received the copy Lange had promised her. She says she would have preferred it had never been taken.

In 1983, ill, her children ask for help with her care. More than thirty thousand dollars arrive. Florence dies in September, shortly after turning eighty.

There is one more thing. In 1939 the thumb that was visible in the lower right of the negative was erased, on Lange’s instructions. Roy Stryker, her boss, disagreed: to him it was a retouch that undermined the credibility of the work.

A photograph like this does two things at once. It helps a country look poverty in the face, and it takes forever the face of a person who did not decide to become a symbol. I don’t think we have to choose between the two. I think we have to remember both.

Here it is. Look at the hands, and look where she looks.
