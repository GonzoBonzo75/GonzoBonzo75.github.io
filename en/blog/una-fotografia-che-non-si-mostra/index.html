Can you see a photograph that isn’t there?

10 November 2026

On Tuesdays, one photograph. The first is one I cannot show you.

December 1971, Minamata, Japan. A small bathroom, the kind found in a family home. A mother, Ryoko, holds her daughter Tomoko, fifteen, in the water. The girl’s body is twisted, still: the mercury that the Chisso factory dumped into the bay marked her before she was even born. It was Ryoko who agreed to be photographed, who suggested the bathroom, who helped set the scene.

The shutter was pressed by William Eugene Smith, an American photographer who had come to Minamata with his wife Aileen to tell of a poisoning many did not want to talk about. Chisso’s men beat him so badly that he risked losing the sight in one eye. He stayed.

The photograph appeared in Life on 2 June 1972 and went around the world. It gave a face to a disease, to a fight for compensation, to a word, Minamata, that until then meant nothing to anyone.

Tomoko died in 1977, aged twenty-one. In 1997, twenty years later, a French production company asked the family for permission to use the photograph in a documentary about the images of the twentieth century. Her father, Yoshio Kamimura, said no: he did not want his daughter’s image to keep working for someone else. The photograph was withdrawn from circulation and, in 1998, Aileen Smith handed the copyright to the family, so that the family would decide.

Behind that refusal were years of gossip in town: some said the family was profiting, others lived off the factory and did not want to hear about compensation. Every time the photo returned in a magazine, it returned to their kitchen too.

For years we called it “Tomoko Uemura in Her Bath”. It was a misreading: the surname was Kamimura. This too says something about how we look at famous photographs: we remember the image, and of the names we almost only keep the sound.

Aileen Smith said that continuing to show it against the family’s wishes would be a profanation. I cannot say whether there is a rule. I know that a photograph is born from a meeting between the one who looks and the one who is looked at, and that sometimes that meeting ends, and it ends when the one who was looked at decides.

That is why there is no photograph above these words. It is not an absence: it is the most honest way I have found to talk about it. And if you know it, you can see it anyway. Perhaps it is right that it stays there, in your mind, and not on a screen.

Sometimes the most right thing a photographer can do with an image is to know when to stop showing it.
