Odyssey (Ὀδύσσεια)

13 November 2026

I have a thing for naming things, and at some point I stopped resisting. My computers, my drives and my camera all carry the name of someone from the Odyssey, which is really the story of a man who leaves, gets lost and finds his way home: more or less what I do with every photograph.

It did not start as a project. One day I noticed I was lining up computers, drives and camera the way you line up a team, and a team needs names. The Odyssey has wonderful ones, and above all each of them already tells a story.

Argo (Ἀργώ) is my oldest Mac, a 2020 MacBook Pro, and it goes out into the square. For the pedants: it is the ship of the Argonauts, which the Odyssey mentions almost in passing, not Odysseus’ dog, whose name is written differently (Ἄργος) and who waits for him for twenty years. It is the lightest, with little software and nothing superfluous, because in the square it is the one that risks the most and I do not want to trust it with more than necessary.

Odysseus (Ὀδυσσεύς) is the M1, the one for missions, which leaves when there is a long stretch away and you have to manage far from home. It used to be called Ulisse; then I decided the names should all be Greek and changed it, and that is when I renamed a few of the drives too.

Ithaca (Ἰθάκη), the M5, is the base. It goes nowhere: it is where I come back to and where the images come back to, to fall into order and stay. Every journey ends there, and the longest part of the work begins right after.

Then there is Hermes (Ἑρμῆς), the NAS. It never leaves and never travels, but it sits on the network and serves all the others, and its job is exactly the messenger’s: carrying things from one machine to another, from one world to another.

The camera is Athena (Ἀθηνᾶ). It is a Leica Q3 with a 28 mm and nothing else, which makes me stand inside the scene. It has one lens and one gaze, never the same twice. In the poem Athena is the one who shows up at the decisive moments and gives the right advice, and I hope mine does the same. I say hope, because the right moment also has to be earned.

The drives are the part I like best, maybe because time lives in there. Penelope (Πηνελόπεια) is the biggest, four terabytes, and it is the general archive. It used to be called Arcadia, but Penelope suits it better: she waits, keeps everything in order and loses nothing, like the other Penelope who wove by day and unwove by night. And honestly, I spend a lot of time sorting and tidying too.

Then there are Telemachus (Τηλέμαχος), Poseidon (Ποσειδῶν), Hera (Ἥρα) and Calypso (Καλυψώ), the emergency drives. Poseidon and Hera were first Nettuno and Giunone, Roman names, but if the story is Greek they have to be too. I take them along when I cannot bring the laptop: I pick one, plug it into my phone and transfer everything straight onto it, so the photographs are safe even far from home. A small fleet in reserve, travelling light.

There are eleven SD cards, like the ships the Laestrygonians sink from Odysseus, except mine usually come back. They carry the names of his companions and of the people around him: Eurylochus (Εὐρύλοχος), the cautious one, and Polites (Πολίτης), the dearest; Perimedes (Περιμήδης), who ties him to the mast, and so has the write-protect switch; Elpenor (Ἐλπήνωρ), forgotten on the roof, who almost always ends up at the bottom of the bag. Then Eumaeus (Εὔμαιος) and Philoetius (Φιλοίτιος), the two faithful ones, and Mentor (Μέντωρ), the guide. Antinous (Ἀντίνοος), the most arrogant of the suitors, is the one that now and then goes haywire. Nausicaa (Ναυσικάα) is the emergency one, the one who saves you on the beach; Circe (Κίρκη) is the one that gets emptied after the backup and starts over; Demodocus (Δημόδοκος), the blind poet, keeps the best stories.

In the bag, next to the Leica, there are also a Moleskine and a pencil. The Moleskine is called Eurycleia (Εὐρύκλεια), like the nurse who recognises Odysseus by his scar: it keeps the names, the times and the phrases overheard in the crowd, and forgets nothing. The pencil does not need a name: it does its job, and that is enough.

It is a game, but it works better than I thought. Saying “I’m leaving with Odysseus” or “this one goes on Penelope” is already a small story, and every photograph makes the same one. We go, we come back, we begin again.
