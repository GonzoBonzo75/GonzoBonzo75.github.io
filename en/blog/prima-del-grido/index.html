Before the Cry

9 November 2026

There is a moment before every celebration that nobody photographs. It isn’t the roar and it isn’t the tears: it’s the afternoon. In Naples, on 4 May 2023, it was the colour of waiting.

Thirty-three years is a long time. It is a whole life for those who counted them one by one, and a family tale for those too young to remember them. In those hours the city seemed suspended, like a breath held in the throat so as not to break the spell.

I walked into the alleys as the light fell slanting between the balconies. Scarves were already around necks, flags already strung from one window to the next like the washing of a hope thirty-three years long. Every wall had a face, and every face had a Diego.

Then someone lit a flare and the air turned sky blue. Then green. Then pink. A man in a blue wig raised his arms the way you do in front of the sea; another lifted his shirt to show his saint, inked on his skin. Nobody knew each other, and everyone recognised each other.

In the middle of that smoke I saw a mother with her little girl in her arms. The child, wearing a mask over her eyes like Osimhen’s, the idol of those days, held her arm stretched upward with a flare pouring out red and green like a torch, while her mother laughed with her whole face, as if there were nothing else around. It is a beautiful memory, one of the most beautiful I carry from that day. I thought that was the real Scudetto: not a trophy, but a little girl who will one day say she was there, even though she won’t remember a thing.

Waiting has a light all of its own. Not the golden light of postcard sunsets, but a dirty, restless light, full of smoke, that gets into your eyes and makes them water before there is even a reason. It is the light of people about to be happy who are still afraid to believe it.

Photographing in those hours is like walking inside a collective prayer. You don’t look for the decisive moment: you wait for it to notice you. The camera becomes an ear more than an eye, and what it hears is a single breath, thousands of people’s, held.

By night the breath became a shout, and the shout, for some, tears. But I hold the afternoon dear: the moment when a whole city still doesn’t know it has won, and that is why it is beautiful.

That is why I start here, from an afternoon and not from a victory. Stories, like photographs, hold more truth when you look at them a moment before they happen. Maybe the best photographs are always the ones from a moment before.
